# Personal introduction to my early experience with LLMs as a user (you can skip this part; it is not really useful for the general topic)

<p align="justify">
I start with a short personal history to tell you how I formed my opinions.
</p>

<p align="justify">
At the end of 2022, for a mix of reasons, I was trying to understand what escape routes I had for leaving my postdoc. I had read that, in Europe, they were creating a large consortium called EuroHPC to provide a GPU-based computing infrastructure and that they were going to hire many future unemployed PhDs. I had a look at these GPUs, and I realized that they were extraordinary. A little later, GPT-3 came out for the average user, and an application to work at the Italian EuroHPC center appeared on my phone. So I applied, had some three interviews, received a job offer, and accepted it. After an initial period of "who knows what to do here," I was involved in projects about training AI models for climate and weather and data anonymization.
But the problem was actually starting to work, given that I was not even able to submit a "job" to run code on this infrastructure. I turned to extensive use of GPT-3 to make some contributions to the projects. It was frustrating because I was running things without understanding or knowing anything about them. But by dividing my tasks into small enough pieces and delegating them to GPT-3, I could accomplish advanced engineering tasks. Many people around me did not realize that I was more of a prompter than an engineer, since I was doing what they were doing. At that time, GPT was not the astonishing entity it is now, so most people did not take it seriously or use it, particularly if they felt they were experts in what they were doing. I am telling this because, from the beginning, I always had the impression that it was something big and not a "parrot" (as an expert in stochastic modelling ironically said at the time). I could not have done anything close to this with Google; I felt that GPT could adapt to my requests.
</p>

<p align="justify">
Later, at the beginning of 2025, I came back to live in Portugal for a job mainly in teaching. In the meantime, in the middle of 2026, I had the opportunity to take a new job in mathematical research. So I came back to mathematical research at a point similar to where I was in 2023 with my new job. </p>


# Living with AI will be a brain teaser (and a danger) for mathematicians, but it will not damage mathematics itself

<p align="justify">
Of course, running all those computational resources and consuming all that energy to solve a math problem is wise, and it was a demonstration of the force of what the model can do, but these are temporary intermediate steps for Big Tech companies. Indeed, the AI supermathematician, after the AI supercode, is just an intermediate step toward running future autonomous AI labs across the sciences. So mathematicians will be able to take a breath soon. I also allow myself to critique the Fields Medalists' letter: they have never been so ready to defend mathematical science from the risks of the current academic environment (in my direct experience, I have heard only two well-known mathematicians openly criticize the behavior of current mathematicians in public). For most PhD students, it is not true that academia is a place where they can flourish with all those opportunities to learn from their seniors; nor is current academia a place whose primary goals are to plant new ideas and understand nature.
On the contrary, I will argue that AI can lead to many connections between different parts of mathematics that we do not expect, and we can learn by using it. It is also true that, in the long run, as has happened various times in history when technology changed work, we could lose some skills.
</p>

<p align="justify">
(Ironically, one of the Fields Medalists was nominated as a strategic consultant for his country's scientific and technological strategy, yet considered AI (in the sense of LLMs) uninteresting and irrelevant. Years earlier, OpenAI and DeepMind had declared their intention to create AGI.)
</p>

## The ways of working with AI

<p align="justify">
I think there are healthy ways and sick ways to work with AI. Now it is possible to adopt two ways of working with AI. The "traditional" one, which is the most widely used and known, is to work with AI as a peer through a chat. If you take the time to figure out what it says and proceed step by step, I do not see this as very different from working with a peer, exchanging information and learning from each other. A typical way AI will help you is something like this: I need to find a bound, the usual inequalities in my field do not work, and I have no idea who or what could help, or whom to ask (or who would even be willing to hear about my problems). I ask AI, and it finds, who knows where, an inequality that helps. It has happened to me that it found inequalities from analytic number theory to provide the bound, which, of course, I would not have found. Also, in my experience, it is very easy to get references to cite from AI, so I do not agree that using AI means you will not cite those who deserve it (unless you do not want to). On the contrary, with its encyclopedic knowledge, your work will have a much better chance of surviving over time and being used when you do not expect it, in some distant future.
This mode is also great for understanding literature (like reading a paper or a book) that is outside your background but that you would like to explore in order to merge it with your research and establish new bridges.
</p>

<p align="justify">
I do not see this way of working as a threat to mathematics.
</p>

<p align="justify">
The sick way would be to try to use an autonomous agentic AI workflow to produce papers in series in one shot. Namely, you connect your model's app to your folders and build a harness (i.e., a structure in which AI can work for a long time while pursuing a goal without getting lost). You can read a short guide on how to use this mode <a href="https://github.com/leodecarlo/How-to-adopt-an-agentic-AI-workflow-for-Mathematics">here</a> if interested. With sharping the prompter ability and the improvements in AI models it is already possible to one-shot papers, even very technical.  But indeed the Fields here they are right that wise to write document that you can not communicate. For this I say "sick" mode. Still this mode can be very usefull to explore idea and many scenarios. For example, in a recent work I am writing we a collegue we arrived at the conclusion that a type of unconventional  models of thermodynamics was not physical, the implication of the result and the math themselg I think were already interesting, but he work was something incomplete in showing that it was possible to fix the problem. I used this mode listing  different modifications to explore if one could lead to a cure for the model, in this process after having got an idea on which was the modelling bottleneck I added to the harness a new attempt to do and I got a very interesting result( which would have been beyond my technical capabilities). It is true that AI now it is coauthor but at the same the meaning of the paper is much more complete and interesting. It worths to remark that when I asked the AI how to modify the thermodynamics ensembles, they started to repeat literature connected to my problem but that was approaching the problem with other philosophies. So AI was great to make the computations, but they  could not have the idea/intuition about which new path to take.
</p>

## What current AI lacks along its builder and what might be future jobs in Mathematics

What I heard looking interview by its builder, while they think they can have entities that will surpass human ability to perform analytical computations (and from my direct experience I believe), they say that they have no idea how to train AI "to be Einstein", that is having the immagination to make gravity theory from an analogy between a person a in elevator and the motion of Sun and Earth. For this a real benchmark would creating a datasets with human knowledge until 1900 and, during the evolution of AI models, asking to make a new theory of gravity. 
So probably immagination, a general view of the field and personal culture will acquire more value with respect an technical hyperspecialization, given the off-loading technical parts of the work. 

Probably the greatest skill of AI in mathematics is the ability to act as a sort of collective intellegince, putting  together the pieces of all we have done so far, a job that would not be possible unless all world mathematicians would decide to stop writing papers to work on collecting together their pieces of information. So if a great goal of Mathematics is the synthesis AI is the perfect tool to do it. On this I suggest reading this article by professional Mathematicians [article](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/).

(I thanks William D. Wick for discussions on the next paragraphs)

Math can be divide crudely into pure Math and applied Mathematics. The pure  Mathematics is realm of pure ideal object and structure, where nobody argues
about axioms for the integers or geometry,  and it  isn't a branch of philosophy, where we prize deep understanding,  it's about getting to the answer.
And I think it is false that AI solving problem on the long term will forbid the development of new Mathematics in the future, on the contrary our continuotion interactions with AI create new precious high quality datasets, that already passed a sort of review and will be reviewed again. This process will make transition AI from imitating our Math to creating its own new Mathematics (exactly as happend in Go AI will pass from imitating human moves to make its own new moves that were not written in any Go book.) 
So pure Mathematicians have real brain teaser to deal with and also to convince new students to get in. But I think they can still have a job if adapt, of course they can not pretend to demolish Data Centers. Pure Mathematicians are used to work mostly individually or small group,  the possible future that I see is that they become a sort of Mathematics Developers, i.e. making variegated larger groups that establish which should an interesting question where different branches of mathematics are expected to meet, formulate the problem clearly and create the right harness for the AI (see <a href="https://github.com/leodecarlo/How-to-adopt-an-agentic-AI-workflow-for-Mathematics">here</a>), once the AI has done start to review the output and eventually iterate until they consider it worths.

Then we have  applied mathematics, which is has two steps: first postulating a model or theory, then producing the predictions from the equations. Einstein, Kuhn and others argued that there is no "mechanical" procedure to build a theory but rather it depends on the
poser's imagination, history, personality, etc. That's how it differs from pure mathematics, where nobody argues
about axioms for the integers or geometry. 


Here is what I would say at your meeting, if I were asked:

Pure mathematics, as a career, is finished. Dead. Attempting to justify its continued existence
by claiming, as did the Fields Medalists recently, that they can still be relevant because they have
a deep philosophical understanding of math won't fly. That would render mathematics as a branch of philosophy.
Nobody is likely to give grant support to a big bunch of new philosophers. Nor will students sign up for their
classes.

Applied mathematics will likely remain as a profession, because it is a two-step activity, and the first step is
the most important: choosing the axioms, or assumptions, for the model. This step involves,
as Thomas Kuhn pointed out in 1962, the theorist's or modeler's background, personality,
biases, and imagination. And controversy about these assumptions is inevitable. By contrast,
Pure math might be characterized
as the only discipline in which no controversy arises about axioms (e.g., nobody argues over what is an integer,
or a sphere). Conceivable, model choice will remain a human activity. The second step---solving the equations, in order to discover the predictions of the model---may be turned over to the AI assistants.
I like the assertion that the bots have no imagination.
I think creativity is largely a function of personal history, plus curiosity. I'm reading a biography of Francis Crick (of DNA fame). He met essentially everybody in the field before arriving at the structure. Einstein is a bit harder to understand, as he mostly worked alone. (Except for his collaboration with his first wife.) Still, he presumably read all the European authors who wrote about Maxwell or thermodynamics. The bots have done the reading; but do they have the curiosity?
And the drive (incentives)?

### Where AI Labs are wrong or not telling the true

AI is incredible, it will accelerate things in labs where running many experiments is an advantage, OpenAI is advertising the acceleration of scientific discoveries it will help with, like dark matter or controlled  fusion. But these guys are not figuring out that AI will help neither with dark matter nor with controlled fusion. The first does not exist; it is the most ad hoc term ever postulated in science. The second is probably not even theoretically possible.

 AI cannot experience the real world, and it does not have genuine imagination. These are not math problems that you can define in a circumscribed way, and they are not BioLabs that you can automate to run many experiments. Moreover, AI is made to please you, not to tell you that what you are doing is a bunch of idiocies, so it cannot really help with these things.
